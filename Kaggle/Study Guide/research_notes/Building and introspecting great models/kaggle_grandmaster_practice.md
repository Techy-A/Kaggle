# Kaggle Grandmaster Practice: How Winning Solutions Are Built, Validated and Introspected

Scope note: these notes draw mainly on first-hand Kaggle write-ups. I pulled their raw markdown through Kaggle's discussion API, so the quotes and numbers below are the authors' own words. Other sources are NVIDIA KGMoN blog posts, the mlcontests.com "State of ML Competitions" reports (2023, 2024, 2025), the Leash Bio post-mortem on BELKA, a Software Engineering Daily transcript with Chris Deotte and Jean-François Puget (CPMP), and FastML's original adversarial-validation article. Each section ends with what it means for (1) CASMI 2026 MS/MS retrieval (MRR@25, about 40-50% novel test molecules) and (2) the 18-class LLM-authorship classifier (balanced accuracy, about 2-3 passages per prompt in test).

---

## Q1. Recurring patterns in 1st-place write-ups: CV that tracks LB, adversarial validation, shake-ups, pseudo-labeling, distillation, diverse ensembles, post-processing

### Takeaway
Almost every winning write-up first shows that the local CV is correlated with the LB. They get there by choosing the right group key, not by using the most folds. With that in place, the score gains come from data (synthetic or external data, pseudo-labels), from distilling large teachers into models that fit the inference budget, from ensembles judged by what they add to the blend rather than by single-model score, and from post-processing tuned to the test distribution. Shake-ups mostly hit teams that tuned to the public LB or had no trustworthy CV.

### Cited Findings

**CV ↔ LB correlation is the first thing winners establish**
- Team Hydrogen (Feedback Prize – Effectiveness, 1st, 2022): "Throughout this competition we had near perfect correlation between CV and LB." Folds were an effectiveness-stratified split on essays. They never judged a model on a single seed: "we thus only relied on checking blends of 3 seeds for each model". They also "always subbed a blend of 3 seeds of models trained on the full data." — [Team Hydrogen 1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/feedback-prize-effectiveness/discussion/347536)
- Feedback Prize – ELL 1st (Dracarys, 2022): "near perfect correlation between ensemble CV and LB" using MultilabelStratifiedKFold. The rule for adding a model was "We only add a model to our ensemble if it improves both our Ensemble CV/LB." — [ELL 1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/feedback-prize-english-language-learning/discussion/369457)
- LECR 1st (Ahmet Erdem, 2023): final validation was 0.764 against public LB 0.727. "While it is a big gap, improvements in my validation score were almost always correlated with LB. And I got my validation score as my Private LB score". The point is that a constant offset is fine as long as the two move together. — [LECR 1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/learning-equality-curriculum-recommendations/discussion/394812)
- LMSYS 1st (sayoulala, 2024): "the CV and LB in this competition were very consistent, making it a rare and excellent contest". 5-fold CVs were reported per fold: Qwen2-72B 0.869–0.881, Llama3-70B 0.873–0.877, distilled Gemma2-9B 0.858–0.876. — [LMSYS 1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/lmsys-chatbot-arena/discussion/527629)
- Eedi 1st (Raja Biswas, 2024) on picking the group key: folds by `QuestionId` "produced overly optimistic estimates with a large gap between validation and leaderboard scores". GroupKFold on `SubjectId` was "overly pessimistic". GroupKFold on `ConstructId` "yielded a narrow Validation/LB gap and good correlation up to ~0.62 public LB score range." He developed on folds 1-4 and validated on fold 0. Synthetic data was tagged "fold 99" and only ever used for training. Final models were trained on all data with the same hyperparameters. — [Eedi 1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688)
- Chris Deotte's four tips (Google Cloud Next 2025): "setting up a robust local validation"; GPU-accelerate the pipeline "to run experiments as fast as possible"; "analyzing and addressing differences between train and test data"; "understanding the competition metric and optimizing your solution for it." — [NVIDIA blog: Kaggle Grandmasters Unveil Winning Strategies](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/kaggle-grandmasters-unveil-winning-strategies-for-data-science-superpowers/)
- CPMP (Jean-François Puget): "with a baseline, create a cross-validation setup and try different baseline models, submit to the competition to see if my cross-validation correlates with the score. If it does, then I don't need to submit too much work with my local setting." — [SED 1817 transcript](https://softwareengineeringdaily.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/SED1817-Chris-Deotte-and-Jean-Francois-Puget.txt)
- KGMoN Playbook (Onodera, Viel, Titericz, Deotte, Sept 2025): "If you can't trust your validation score, you're flying blind." "Match your CV strategy to how the test data is structured", e.g. TimeSeriesSplit for time data and GroupKFold for users or patients. — [NVIDIA: Kaggle Grandmasters Playbook](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)

**Train/test shift checks and adversarial validation**
- The Playbook names "Train vs. test distribution checks" as a step "many people miss". Its example is the Amazon KDD Cup '23 winners, who "uncovered both a train—test distribution shift and temporal patterns in the target—insights that shaped the final approach." — [NVIDIA Playbook](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- The original adversarial-validation method (Zygmunt Zając, FastML, 2016): label train rows 1 and test rows 0, then train a classifier to tell them apart. ROC AUC around 0.5 means no shift. On Kaggle Santander Customer Satisfaction he got LR 49.82%, RF(10) 50.05% and RF(100) 49.95%, so the sets were indistinguishable, and validation matched private LB (RF 75.32% validation against 74.37% private). When AUC is high, the proposal is to take the train rows that look most like test as the validation set. — [KDnuggets / FastML: Adversarial Validation, Explained](https://www.kdnuggets.com/2016/10/adversarial-validation-explained.html)
- Tooling exists. For example, `nyaggle` has an `adversarial_validate` function that returns the AUC and feature importances. — [nyaggle on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/nyaggle)
- The Kaggle Book (Banachewicz & Massaron, 2022) has a chapter, "Designing Good Validation", covering overfitting, shake-ups, leakage, adversarial validation, validation strategies and "strategies for your final submissions." The second edition adds Bojan Tunguz as co-author (per the search-result summary; I could not read the chapter text). — [Packt: Designing good validation](https://packtpub.com/en-jp/product/the-kaggle-book-9781801817479/chapter/designing-good-validation-8/section/designing-good-validation-8)
- David Austin (NVIDIA GM) spends the first days of every competition visualizing data. In NLP he projects embeddings to low dimensions with t-SNE or UMAP. In one satellite competition this "gave insight into the different distribution of images, which turned out to be real versus synthetically generated." — [NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/kaggle-grandmasters-unveil-winning-strategies-for-data-science-superpowers/)
- In BELKA, a Kaggler (Hideaki Ogasawara) plotted the test chemical space. It showed kinase0 sitting in an entirely separate region from the triazine train set, which is the visual version of adversarial validation. — [Leash Bio: BELKA results](https://leashbio.substack.com/p/belka-results-suggest-computers-can)

**Shake-ups**
- Abdullah Meda (DAIGT, 8th, 2024): "I feel incredibly blessed to have experienced such a significant shake-up! While I anticipated a slight bump upwards, I did not expect the solution to generalize this well!" His solution used perplexity and GLTR features from GPT-2 variants. — [DAIGT 8th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/llm-detect-ai-generated-text/discussion/470224)
- In BELKA, one participant's retrospective said they "had no reliable cross-validation index and evaluated everything on the public leaderboard, which was so shaken". They finished 508 of 1,982. — [zenn.dev retrospective](https://zenn.dev/yuto_mo/articles/062b6daff6f1c9) (Japanese, secondary)
- The LLM 20 Questions winner (c-number, 2024) said "the leaderboard remained somewhat unstable until the end, and any competitor in the gold zone could have potentially secured the win." His second agent scored 1228.2, which would have placed 4th, against 1259.9 for the winning agent. — [LLM 20 Questions 1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/llm-20-questions/discussion/531106)
- Ruchi Bhatia (triple GM): "simulating the public/private LB split using multiple CV folds to estimate potential leaderboard shakeups. It's a sanity check against overfitting to public scores." — [NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/kaggle-grandmasters-unveil-winning-strategies-for-data-science-superpowers/)

**Pseudo-labeling (leak-free, soft, multi-round)**
- Team Hydrogen ran 3 rounds of pseudo-labeling on the previous Feedback competition's data, "in a leak-free manner for the individual folds and additionally for our models trained on all the data (6 versions of pseudo labels in total)". They used soft labels in two ways: (a) concatenated with the real labels, or (b) pre-training on the pseudo-labels and then fine-tuning on the real labels. — [Team Hydrogen 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/feedback-prize-effectiveness/discussion/347536)
- KGMoN Playbook rules: stronger teachers give better pseudo-labels and multi-round beats single-pass; use soft pseudo-labels; pseudo-labels can be used to denoise labeled data; "When using k-fold, you must compute k sets of pseudo-labels so that validation data never sees labels from models trained on itself." Their example is BirdCLEF 2024. — [NVIDIA Playbook](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- LMSYS 2nd (tascj et al., 2024): the Stage-1 ensemble (gemma-2-9b, gemma-2-27b and ArmoRM-Llama3-8B, ensemble val log-loss 0.876) pseudo-labeled 240k external conversations, and the 9B and 8B models were then retrained on them. — [LMSYS 2nd place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/lmsys-chatbot-arena/discussion/527685)

**Knowledge distillation into the inference budget**
- LMSYS 1st: Llama3-70B and Qwen2-72B were trained with QLoRA in 5 folds, and their OOF logit distributions were distilled into Gemma2-9B. The 5 fold LoRAs were averaged, then quantized to 8-bit with GPTQ, with length-2000 TTA at inference. "distillation is a very promising approach, especially in the current Kaggle competitions, where inference constraints are a limiting factor." — [LMSYS 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/lmsys-chatbot-arena/discussion/527629); also summarized in [mlcontests 2024](https://mlcontests.com/state-of-machine-learning-competitions-2024/)
- In Eedi 1st, two 72B rankers trained only on competition data pseudo-labeled the synthetic MCQs, and those labels were used to train the 14B ranker. That moved private LB from 0.531 to 0.575 (+0.044). — [Eedi 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688)

**Diverse ensembles, judged by blend contribution**
- Team Hydrogen: "single model scores did not correlate well here with their ability to blend into larger ensembles. So a better individual model could have quite a worse performance in the blend, diversity really mattered." One input format was better on its own but "the former approach blended significantly better". Some blend weights were negative and worked on both CV and LB. Second-level LightGBM and NN stackers added "about 0.003-0.005 points on CV and the leaderboard throughout the competition." — [Team Hydrogen 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/feedback-prize-effectiveness/discussion/347536)
- KGMoN: hill climbing starts from the best model and greedily adds models and weights while validation improves. It won Predict Calorie Expenditure with XGB, CatBoost, NN and linear models. A 3-level stack won Podcast Listening Time. Ensembling XGBoost across 100 seeds and retraining on 100% of the data gave further gains in Predicting Optimal Fertilizers (MAP@3). — [NVIDIA Playbook](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- DAIGT 1st (Broad, Biswas, Bamba, 2024): they ensembled on ranks, not raw scores ("The minimum scoring text gets a rank of 1 ... These ranks are averaged"), with "minor weighting ... based on public LB and intuition". The most weight went to Mistral-7B. — [DAIGT 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/llm-detect-ai-generated-text/discussion/473295)

**Post-processing tuned to the metric or test distribution**
- Team Hydrogen: "The log loss metric is always only optimal if the mean prediction per column matches the mean of the target columns". After each model they fitted scaling factors to match the train mean and then applied them on the LB. — [Team Hydrogen 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/feedback-prize-effectiveness/discussion/347536)
- LECR 1st: "Postprocessing was very important." It used relative probabilities, i.e. the gap to the top match, with separate rules for train-seen and unseen contents: "matching train set contents was like a classification problem, matching test set contents was like an assignment problem." Unseen contents were kept if they were within 5% of the topic's best probability. — [LECR 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/learning-equality-curriculum-recommendations/discussion/394812)
- Eedi 3rd (Yi-Chia Chen, 2024): unseen-misconception probabilities were multiplied by a constant C until 75% of first predictions were unseen. Public went 0.590 → 0.658 and private 0.564 → 0.600 (details in Q4/Q6). — [Eedi 3rd](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551498)
- Metric exploits do happen. In LLM Prompt Recovery (2024) the winner and other top teams appended the token "lucrarea", which exploited a quirk of the sentence-embedding metric. — [mlcontests 2024](https://mlcontests.com/state-of-machine-learning-competitions-2024/)

### Inferences
- The group key used for folds is the most important validation decision. Eedi's QuestionId → SubjectId → ConstructId sweep is a template: try several keys, then keep the one whose CV moves with the LB without being systematically too optimistic or too pessimistic.
- A fixed CV-LB offset is fine (LECR: 0.764 vs 0.727). What matters is rank correlation across experiments, so write a CV-vs-LB scatter into the experiment log, as Team Hydrogen did with their published CV/LB plot.

### Gaps
- I found no first-hand write-up giving numbers on adversarial-validation AUC from a recent (2023-2025) text or retrieval competition. The well-documented example is the 2016 Santander one.
- I could not get exact public-vs-private rank deltas for DAIGT, LMSYS or BELKA. Kaggle's leaderboard API call failed and the leaderboards render client-side.

## Q2. How grandmasters document experiments: ablation tables, "what worked / what didn't", one-change-at-a-time, experiment loops

### Takeaway
Top write-ups follow a fixed shape: tl;dr, then validation scheme, then pipeline, then a staged ablation table where each change is credited a private-LB delta, then "what didn't work". The workflow behind that is a baseline-first, fast-iteration loop where each tested change becomes the new baseline only if CV improves, which is CPMP's "experimental science" framing.

### Cited Findings
- CPMP: "I see data science and machine learning as an experimental science, just like physics. Ideally, to build a good model, you have a baseline ... we design and experiment to test if the change is really improving. Then you run it and you look at the results. Depending on the result, it becomes your new baseline". On prioritizing: "you estimate the time it takes to code it and run it ... after years of doing this every week, we get some feeling of what might work first." — [SED 1817](https://softwareengineeringdaily.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/SED1817-Chris-Deotte-and-Jean-Francois-Puget.txt)
- Chris Deotte: "First, we explore the data, next we build the standard baseline model ... Finally, we create a local validation scheme and compute the baseline's validation metric score ... Determining how to beat the baseline is the difficult task. This requires us to understand the data deeply through EDA and understand the data by observing how our baseline model behaves ... We repeat this process as fast as possible!" — [NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/kaggle-grandmasters-unveil-winning-strategies-for-data-science-superpowers/)
- Deotte on error analysis as introspection: "I've been in 80 competitions in the last six years ... I'll train a model. I'll look at its output. It'll always be getting something wrong. I oftentimes know exactly where to look". He likens training a model to teaching a student. — [SED 1817](https://softwareengineeringdaily.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/SED1817-Chris-Deotte-and-Jean-Francois-Puget.txt)
- Deotte's automated feature search: overnight loops that combined random groups of 2-6 of the 23 columns, target-encoded them with cuDF, and kept a combination only if validation improved. "I recently won a competition doing just that" (Kaggle Playground, insurance premiums). — [SED 1817](https://softwareengineeringdaily.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/SED1817-Chris-Deotte-and-Jean-Francois-Puget.txt); also cited in the [NVIDIA Playbook](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- KGMoN: "The biggest lever we have in any competition or real-world project is the number of high-quality experiments we can run." On re-running baselines: "Re-running baselines after data changes can reveal whether you're making progress—or uncover problems like leakage." In Rainfall, an un-engineered GBDT+NN+SVR baseline ensemble took 2nd, and "even a single Support Vector Classifier (SVC) baseline would have placed near the top". — [NVIDIA Playbook](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- Hypothesis-driven planning (Eedi 1st): the write-up opens with a "Meta Thoughts" list of upfront hypotheses: synthetic data helps OOD; distillation from large teachers; CoT distillation; quantization calibration matters; full training might beat LoRA. "These hypotheses guided me to design my experiments throughout the competition and helped me to stick to an idea even though the initial attempts were not successful." — [Eedi 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688)
- The canonical staged ablation (Eedi 1st, 14B reranker, private LB): few-shot examples in context 0.495 → 0.531 (+0.036); pseudo-labeled synthetic data from 72B teachers → 0.575 (+0.044); negatives per positive 16 → 24 plus 2x synthetic → 0.596 (+0.021); CoT added to 50% of inputs → 0.615 (+0.019); adding the 32B reranker stage → 0.625 (+0.010); adding the 72B listwise stage → 0.638 (+0.013). There was also a separate calibration-dataset ablation for AWQ quantization. — [Eedi 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688)
- "What didn't work" lists:
  - Eedi 1st retriever: iterative hard-negative mining; larger batch via cross-device negatives; same-SubjectId batching; converting LLM retrievers to bidirectional encoders (NV-Embed style). Reranker: LoRA merging via mergekit; naive QwQ-32B fine-tuning. — [Eedi 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688)
  - BELKA 1st: complex tokenization (bi/tri-grams, proper atomInSmiles); models deeper than 6 layers or wider than 32; multi-input SMILES+fingerprint models; "Pre-training on a larger dataset—I spent about a month experimenting with ZINC"; custom losses; gated fusion of building blocks. — [BELKA 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/leash-BELKA/discussion/519020)
  - Team Hydrogen: augmentations "as always they were not really helpful for NLP finetuning", except mask augmentation, which helped ensemble diversity. "Hyperparameter tuning was very important though". — [Team Hydrogen 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/feedback-prize-effectiveness/discussion/347536)
  - Feedback Prize 2021 1st: quantified items such as "adversarial learning (awp/fgm): cv increase 0.01, lb 5fold ensemble increase 0.003" and "single model lb 0.691, model ensemble 0.706". — [FP2021 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/feedback-prize-2021/discussion/313177)
- Per-model CV / Public / Private tables are standard. For example, ELL 1st lists every ensemble member's CV, public and private score (deberta-v3-large 0.4460 / 0.4368 / 0.4346 ...). — [ELL 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/feedback-prize-english-language-learning/discussion/369457)
- Ruchi Bhatia: "Save intermediate features and predictions. Treat it like a production ML pipeline", "Use lightweight scripts for debugging, heavy models only for final runs", and read forums "like research papers". — [NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/kaggle-grandmasters-unveil-winning-strategies-for-data-science-superpowers/)
- In LMSYS 1st the CV/LB section reports only the final configuration: "There were too many experiments before, but these results are the most important." Winners keep the full log internally and publish a summary. — [LMSYS 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/lmsys-chatbot-arena/discussion/527629)
- Eedi 1st shows "live eval" curves during fine-tuning, i.e. metric tracked during training. — [Eedi 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688)

### Inferences
- Steal the Eedi 1st format for our own docs: a cumulative ablation table (change → Δ metric on the held-out split, plus Δ on LB where available), a separate retriever table (MAP and recall@K), and a "didn't work" list. It is easy to write as you go if each experiment changes one thing against the current baseline.
- Saving OOF predictions for every run is what makes hill-climbing, stacking and blend-contribution checks possible later, and Hydrogen's "check models in the blend even if weak individually" depends on it.

### Gaps
- I found no first-hand source on specific tracker tools (W&B, MLflow, spreadsheets) used by Deotte, Psi or Bojan Tunguz. The interviews I found (e.g. Psi in [Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/meet-the-data-scientist-who-just-cannot-stop-winning-on-kaggle-dfc0e6fe88f8)) give only general advice: "get your hands dirty, don't be afraid to fail".
- I could not access the Kaggle Book chapter text for its exact recommendations.

## Q3. Text competitions relevant to LLM authorship: DAIGT (2024), LMSYS (2024), Eedi, LLM 20 Questions, Feedback Prize

### Takeaway
In DAIGT, the data mix decided the winner, not the architecture. The 1st-place team said each of their single models was already gold-level because of a large, diverse generated corpus from many LLMs, decoding settings and adversarial augmentations. Close runners-up added test-time adaptation: test-specific tokenizers, MLM on test text, pseudo-labeling, or short-context student models. Introspection was done through perplexity/token-probability features (Ghostbuster, GLTR) and by asking what wording quirks the models were picking up. LMSYS showed that distilling large teachers into a 9B model wins when inference is constrained.

### Cited Findings

**LLM – Detect AI Generated Text (DAIGT), Jan 2024**
- 1st (Nicholas Broad, Raja Biswas, Udbhav Bamba). tl;dr: "The hosts did not reveal much information about what the generated essays would be like, so we aimed to create a diverse set of generated essays ... The modeling approach was less important, as we had multiple single models in the 0.970+ range due to the quality of the dataset." — [DAIGT 1st comprehensive write-up](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/llm-detect-ai-generated-text/discussion/473295)
  - Data mix, built iteratively: "For each datamix iteration, we attempted to plug blindspots of the previous generation models". Human text: the full Persuade corpus (all 15 prompts) plus ELLIPSE, NarrativeQA, Wikipedia, Brown, IMDB and GPT-2 output. Generated text came from (1) proprietary LLMs (gpt-3.5, gpt-4, claude, cohere, gemini, palm), (2) open LLMs (llama, falcon, mistral, mixtral), (3) existing datasets (DAIGT-v2, OUTFOX, Ghostbuster, T5 MLM essays), and (4) LLMs fine-tuned on PERSUADE. Fine-tuning used instruction tuning on metadata (prompt, score, ELL status, grade), "one topic held out" (generate only the held-out prompt "to encourage new writing styles") and span-wise generation.
  - Decoding diversity: contrastive search, guidance scale, typical_p, suppress_tokens, high temperature, large top-k, fill-in-the-blank (MLM-like) prompting, prompting with and without source texts, and rewriting.
  - Adversarial augmentations: spelling correction, character insertion/deletion/swap, synonym replacement, obfuscations, back-translation, random capitalization, sentence swap. A heavily normalized copy of the data (lowercased, special characters stripped) was used for an extra model, to "learn deeper patterns" and resist character attacks.
  - Best models were trained on 160k samples (40k human). Models: Mistral-7B QLoRA (r=64, α=16, SEQ_CLS on q/k/v/o); deberta-v3-large classification with continuous labels ("essays generated with instruction-tuned LLMs assigned less than 1 score"); deberta-v3-small with a custom tokenizer, MLM on train+test, and test pseudo-labels; deberta-v3-large with pairwise ranking loss; Ghostbuster (token probabilities from Llama-7B and TinyLlama-1.1B plus unigram/trigram models, 10 aggregate operations over each probability stream and their ratios giving 100 features, then SVM+RF); and Ahmet's unsupervised neighbor approach. The ensemble was a rank average.
- Tokenizer trick, from a public discussion that the 1st place cites: the leading public approach was "basic tf-idf ngrams combined with robust models from sklearn", and "most information gain arises from distribution differences, often induced by human or artificially introduced typos." Training a BPE tokenizer on the corpus let typos, stems and suffixes become vocabulary items without blowing up the vocabulary the way char n-grams do. — [Ertuğrul Demir, "Using Custom Tokenizers to Boost Your Score"](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/llm-detect-ai-generated-text/discussion/458522)
- 5th (James Day): fine-tuned deberta-v3-large and mamba-790m on about 1.7M examples. The mix was mostly Pile and SlimPajama document completions from 14 open LLMs (Llama-2 up to 70B, Mistral, Falcon, MPT, CodeLlama, WizardLM ...), with randomized decoding (temperature 0-2, top-k ∈ {off, 20, 40}, top-p 0.5-1, frequency penalty 0-0.5). PERSUADE was always 1% of the mixture and Pile was 62-99%. At test time a teacher ensemble soft-labels the test set, then 128- and 256-character "short-context student" DeBERTas are fine-tuned on random test chunks and predict over overlapping chunks. His explanation: "the short-context student models learn to look for dataset-specific wording quirks that are correlated with whatever the long-context models look for in the broader documents." Scores: full-context DeBERTa 0.970 private; students 0.977 without Mamba and 0.972 with it. "We didn't pick the best submission as one of our final 3, so we got 5th place instead of 3rd." — [DAIGT 5th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/llm-detect-ai-generated-text/discussion/470093)
- 8th (Abdullah Meda): linguistic features: text- and sentence-level perplexity from GPT-2 variants, plus GLTR rank buckets. Observation: "content generated by LLMs have relatively lower PPLs compared to the text written by humans." He rose sharply in the shake-up. — [DAIGT 8th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/llm-detect-ai-generated-text/discussion/470224)
- mlcontests summary: the DAIGT winner "used a combination of Mistral-7B, DeBERTa, LLama-7B, and Llama-1.1B, along with synthetic data". — [mlcontests 2024](https://mlcontests.com/state-of-machine-learning-competitions-2024/)

**LMSYS – Chatbot Arena Human Preference (2024)**
- 1st (sayoulala): post-pretrain one epoch on the "ut" dataset; 5-fold Llama3-70B and Qwen2-72B teachers (QLoRA r=64, α=128, all linear layers, max_len 1024); distill logits into Gemma2-9B; average the fold LoRAs; GPTQ 8-bit; TTA. Final private 0.96898 (log loss). — [LMSYS 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/lmsys-chatbot-arena/discussion/527629)
- 2nd (tascj, liushuzhi, kapenon): StratifiedGroupKFold grouped by prompt; full-parameter training rather than LoRA. "Full swap" augmentation includes both the original and the A/B-swapped sample, with gradients accumulated in the same optimizer step "to avoid overfitting", for a stable 0.003 gain over random swap. Pseudo-labeled 240k external conversations. — [LMSYS 2nd](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/lmsys-chatbot-arena/discussion/527685)

**Eedi and LLM 20 Questions**
- Eedi winners distilled CoT from Claude 3.5 Sonnet into fine-tuned Qwen2.5 7B/14B/32B "reasoners". The rerankers saw CoT in only 50% of training inputs, "to use external reasoning when available and otherwise rely on its internal reasoning". — [Eedi 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688)
- LLM 20 Questions 1st (c-number): no fine-tuning. He estimated each keyword's prior probability of being in the private list from the public keywords, using word count, English word frequency and "thing-ness" (from GPT-4o-mini Yes/No token probabilities), "based on the observation that the public keyword list closely resembles the private one." He also exploited the "Agent Alpha" alphabetical-bisection protocol shared by other agents. — [LLM 20Q 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/llm-20-questions/discussion/531106); [mlcontests 2024](https://mlcontests.com/state-of-machine-learning-competitions-2024/)

**Feedback Prize series**
- Effectiveness 1st (Team Hydrogen: Babakhin, Singer and others): feeding the whole essay with per-discourse START/END pooling and an auxiliary type loss "made training and inference much faster, but also improved accuracy significantly". Only deberta-(v3)-large backbones worked. — [Team Hydrogen 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/feedback-prize-effectiveness/discussion/347536)
- ELL 1st: many DeBERTa/RoBERTa variants with different pooling (Mean, Concat, WeightedLayer, GeM, LSTM), AWP and layer re-init, plus SVR on frozen embeddings from BART and RoBERTa sentence encoders. Weights were tuned per target with Optuna on OOFs. — [ELL 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/feedback-prize-english-language-learning/discussion/369457)

**Trends**
- 2023: "Decoder-only GPT-style models are starting to disrupt the status quo of DeBERTa"; generative LLMs used to make synthetic data for DeBERTa. — [mlcontests 2023](https://mlcontests.com/state-of-competitive-machine-learning-2023/)
- 2024: DeBERTa was still the encoder of choice; a common pattern was decoder-generated synthetic data used to train encoders (PII Data Detection). — [mlcontests 2024](https://mlcontests.com/state-of-machine-learning-competitions-2024/)
- 2025: "Almost all of the winning solutions we found for competitions with a text-processing element involved either a Qwen2.5 model, a Qwen3 model, or both." — [mlcontests 2025](https://mlcontests.com/state-of-machine-learning-competitions-2025/)

### Inferences (for the 18-class authorship task)
- DAIGT is the closest precedent. Generator diversity (many model families, sampling configs and prompt styles) and robustness augmentations generalized better than architecture changes. For 18-class authorship, the analogue is to cover each class's realistic decoding and prompt variety, and to include obfuscation and normalization variants so the model cannot lean on surface artifacts.
- Ghostbuster/GLTR/perplexity features from several reference LMs, and their ratios, are a cheap, interpretable, diverse ensemble member. They also serve as an introspection tool: which classes separate on token-probability statistics, and which only on style?
- With about 2-3 passages per prompt in test, test-time adaptation is directly applicable: a tokenizer fitted on test text, MLM on test, and teacher→short-context-student on test chunks (James Day). So is aggregating chunk-level predictions. LMSYS-style grouping by prompt in CV (StratifiedGroupKFold) is essential so the same prompt never appears in both train and validation.
- Rank averaging (DAIGT) suits AUC. For balanced accuracy, the analogue is per-class probability calibration and threshold or prior adjustment on OOF predictions, comparable to Hydrogen's train-mean scaling.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve DAIGT 2nd-4th place write-ups (topic IDs unknown) or exact public/private LB figures for DAIGT's shake-up.
- No write-up I found explicitly used attribution or saliency methods to find which tokens drove the classifiers. Introspection in these write-ups was via data-mix ablations, perplexity features and error analysis.

## Q4. Retrieval/ranking competitions relevant to molecule retrieval: LECR (2023), Eedi (2024), BELKA (2024)

### Takeaway
Winners split the problem into high-recall candidate generation followed by increasingly expensive rerankers. They track recall@K for the retriever separately from the end metric, and they will pick a lower-MAP but higher-recall retriever. They union several candidate sources and validate specifically on unseen targets. Eedi's top teams treated novelty as the central problem: synthetic data for unseen classes, separate validation on unseen misconceptions, and an explicit prior boost for unseen candidates.

### Cited Findings

**Eedi – Mining Misconceptions in Mathematics (Dec 2024, MAP@25 over 2.5k+ misconceptions)**
- 1st (Raja Biswas) cascade: retriever keeps the top 32 plus up to 32 more above a dynamic threshold (top score minus a constant) → Qwen2.5-14B pointwise reranker picks the top 8 → Qwen2.5-32B picks the top 5 → Qwen2.5-72B listwise ranks the top 5. The final 25 = ranks 1-5 from 72B, 6-8 from 32B, 9-25 from 14B. — [Eedi 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688)
- Diagnosing recall: "I tracked both `map@25` and `recall@32` metrics and prioritized optimizing for `recall@32`, since recall was more important for overall pipeline performance." Hard negatives and reranker-score distillation "consistently improved `map@25` ... but did not positively impact `recall@32`", so he chose the high-recall Qwen2.5-14B encoder over the best-MAP encoder. The best encoder-only submission scored 0.524 public / 0.475 private. — [Eedi 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688)
- Retriever details: MultipleNegativesRankingLoss, LoRA r=64 on all linear layers, batch 128, 12 epochs, temperature 0.01 (vs. the usual 0.02). "only one demonstration per misconception appeared in each training batch, as multiple demonstrations would introduce label noise through in-batch negatives". Also pretraining on all synthetic data before curation. Reranker: Yes-minus-No logit, 1 positive vs. N negatives with cross-entropy; more negatives (16 → 24) helped. — [Eedi 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688)
- Synthetic data for novelty: grouped generation over clusters of confusable misconceptions, with clusters built "leveraging co-occurrence statistics of misconceptions in retriever/ranker predictions on validation data". The confusion structure of his own model guided data generation. Claude 3.5 Sonnet generated the data and GPT-4o filtered it as an LLM judge. The final set was 1.8k real + 10.6k synthetic MCQs covering 4,791 misconceptions. New LLM-invented misconceptions were deduplicated against the pool with embedding similarity thresholds: matched if >0.995, dropped if 0.95-0.995. Listwise position bias was noted; shuffling candidates as TTA was not run because of time. — [Eedi 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688); the host's summary is in [Eedi blog](https://eedi.com/blog/from-wrong-answers-to-real-insights-how-we-used-a-kaggle-challenge-to-map-student-misconceptions)
- 2nd (RihanPiggy et al.): "misconception augmentation". An LLM wrote an explanation for each candidate misconception, computed offline so it was free at inference, which "significantly boosted retriever performance by about 2-4%". The Llama3.1-70B and Qwen2.5-72B explanations beat GPT-4o-mini's. Last-token pooling beat mean pooling for Qwen. Retriever table: Qwen2.5-14B 0.479-0.485 private, Qwen2.5-32B 0.495, QwQ-32B 0.500. Reranking used sliding windows: 14B on ranks 8-17, then 72B and Llama-3.3-70B on the top 10. — [Eedi 2nd](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551651)
- 4th (Takoi): "Since most misconceptions in the test set were anticipated to be absent in the train set, we focused on improving the accuracy for these unseen misconceptions." They "Evaluated not only the overall fold scores but also the scores for validation data extracted specifically for misconceptions not included in the training data," and tracked retriever top-25 recall (about 0.923-0.926 on real data, about 0.99 on generated data). — [Eedi 4th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551559); [4th place slides](https://speakerdeck.com/rist/4th-place-solution-eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics)
- 3rd (Yi-Chia Chen): found "over 900 unseen misconceptions in the misconceptions table that never appeared in the training data". He used LB probing, predicting one misconception per question: seen-only scored 0.154 and unseen-only 0.444, so he inferred roughly 1:3 seen:unseen in test. He then scaled unseen candidates' probabilities by C until 75% of top-1 predictions were unseen: public 0.590 → 0.658, private 0.564 → 0.600. Shuffling the reranker's candidate order added a little more (private 0.602). — [Eedi 3rd](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551498)

**Learning Equality – Curriculum Recommendations (LECR, 2023)**
- 1st (Ahmet Erdem) pipeline: candidate selection → feature engineering → LightGBM → post-processing. Candidates are the outer join of: char-4gram TF-IDF per language (top 20 above 1% cosine, via sparse_dot_topn); ensembled multilingual transformer embeddings trained with ArcFace (contents as classes, margin rising 0.1 → 0.5 over 22 epochs); "second degree" matches via the nearest train topic's contents; and matches from same-title, same-representation-text and same-parent train topics. — [LECR 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/learning-equality-curriculum-recommendations/discussion/394812)
- The LightGBM reranker used about 27 features: retrieval scores plus max/min per topic, lengths, "is content train", match counts and so on, with a monotonic constraint on cosine distance. For diversity, a second LightGBM "Excluded features which will potentially have different distribution on real test set", a shift-aware design choice. Validation held out about 33% of non-source topics and excluded contents that matched only validation topics, which simulates unseen content. — [LECR 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/learning-equality-curriculum-recommendations/discussion/394812)
- 3rd (heng et al.): a simple holdout of 4,000 (later 1,000) non-source topics "was unexpectedly stable". Recall was measured as "max positive score top50/top100". A retriever ensemble raised top-50 recall to 0.9235 but "basically no change on the LB score", so they used a single retriever. — [LECR 3rd](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/learning-equality-curriculum-recommendations/discussion/394838)

**BELKA – Leash Bio Big Encoded Library for Chemical Assessment (2024)**
- Data: about 133M triazine-core DEL molecules (305/768/951 building blocks at the three positions), 3 targets (BRD4, sEH/EPHX2, HSA/ALB), about 2,000 teams. The test set had three strata: (a) shared building blocks, (b) triazine molecules with non-shared building blocks, and (c) kinase0, a different library with different cores and attachment chemistry that shared nothing with train. — [Leash Bio: BELKA results](https://leashbio.substack.com/p/belka-results-suggest-computers-can)
- Outcome: the winner "does pretty good with shared building blocks ... mostly worse with non-shared building blocks ... and can't predict in new chemical space at all. All of the competitors looked like this or worse." "Three of the winners in the top 5 all used the same tutorial notebook with minimal changes, which suggests the randomness in rerunning the same simple, but noisy, notebook a bunch of times performed better than nearly all of the extremely diligent and fancy approaches". In a top-100 enrichment test on kinase0 (about 120k molecules, about 1,000 hits per target, so random gives about 1/100), the winner got 2/100 for sEH and 0/100 for BRD4 and HSA. — [Leash Bio](https://leashbio.substack.com/p/belka-results-suggest-computers-can)
- 1st (Victor Shlepov): a small SMILES transformer (4 layers, 8 heads, dim 32, 43-token almost character-level vocabulary), pretrained in two stages: (1) MLM on train+test+external data, then (2) SMILES→ECFP (2048-bit, with chirality) prediction with embeddings locked. Fine-tuned on BELKA plus an external DEL dataset with a masked loss. Validation "put aside 3% of blocks from train set so my validation set included molecules with one ore more non-shared blocks - some 9 million of samples". His conclusion: "no magic, just a pure luck and randomness". — [BELKA 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/leash-BELKA/discussion/519020)

### Inferences (for CASMI 2026, MRR@25, about 40-50% novel molecules)
- Use the Eedi/LECR template: high-recall candidate generation (union of formula/mass filters, spectral-similarity retrieval, learned embedding retrieval, and analogue/"second-degree" neighbours), then a cascade of rerankers. Track recall@K of the candidate set (K = cut-off fed to the reranker) as a first-class metric, separate from MRR@25, and keep a higher-recall retriever even when its MRR is lower.
- Validate the way the test is built. Hold out molecules (and ideally scaffolds or Murcko frameworks) not seen in training, as BELKA 1st did with building blocks, Eedi with ConstructId and LECR with unseen contents. Report CV separately for "seen" and "novel" query molecules, as Eedi 4th did with unseen misconceptions.
- Novel test molecules are the Eedi "unseen misconception" problem. Expect a prior mismatch between seen and novel candidates. Eedi 3rd's calibrated boost for unseen candidates (+0.036 private) shows that explicitly modelling the expected novel fraction (here about 40-50%) can be worth more than a model upgrade. Tune it on a CV that matches that novel ratio rather than by LB probing alone.
- BELKA is a warning: models memorized building blocks and failed on truly new chemistry, and the private ranking was partly seed noise. Self-supervised pretraining on all candidate structures, including test candidates (MLM+ECFP in BELKA 1st, MLM on test text in DAIGT), is a cheap generalization lever. Expect noise in close finishes.
- Eedi 2nd's offline enrichment of candidate representations (LLM-written explanations) maps to precomputing rich candidate-side features (predicted spectra, fragment trees, fingerprints) offline, since the candidate pool is known in advance.

### Gaps
- I found no Kaggle competition specifically on MS/MS spectra or molecule identification. The closest chemistry competitions found were BELKA and Open Polymer Prediction 2025 (whose winner, James Day, used AutoGluon + XGB + LGBM + TabM — [mlcontests 2025](https://mlcontests.com/state-of-machine-learning-competitions-2025/)).
- I could not retrieve BELKA 2nd-5th place write-ups or per-split scores (the Leash figures are images).

## Q5. Final-submission selection: one safe and one risky, avoiding public-LB overfitting, the winner's curse

### Takeaway
Winners usually make one pick that follows the CV and one that reflects a hypothesis about the private test, such as a bigger model, a prior shift or a riskier ensemble. Write-ups repeatedly record the pain of not picking the best-CV submission. With three selections allowed, top teams use the extra slot for the submission their CV trusts but the public LB dislikes.

### Cited Findings
- Kaggle's rule: you pick a small number of final submissions (usually 2, sometimes 3) and the best private score among them counts. If you pick none, Kaggle defaults to the highest public scores. — [Kaggle discussion "Is the rule of choosing two submissions the best?"](https://www.kaggle.com/discussions/general/432319); [Medium: Kaggle Handbook, surviving a shake-up](https://medium.com/global-maksimum-data-information-technologies/kaggle-handbook-fundamentals-to-survive-a-kaggle-shake-up-3dec0c085bc8) (search summary; page returned 403)
- Eedi 2nd: "larger models (e.g., 72B parameters) consistently delivered stronger validation scores ... However, these larger models initially performed worse on the Public LB, leading to some concerns. Despite this, we trusted the validation scores and included the 72B model in our final submissions (special thanks to the three-submission rule!). Ultimately, the 72B model produced outstanding Private Leaderboard scores, helping us secure a prize." — [Eedi 2nd](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551651)
- Eedi 1st chose its final retriever for recall, not for the public LB: "For my final submission selections, I chose the high-recall `Qwen/Qwen2.5-14B` encoder (Model 3) instead of the best `map@25` encoder (Model 4)." — [Eedi 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688)
- ELL 1st: "The best CV we get using this approach was 0.44073 (We didn't select this sub fearing overfitting but this has best private score.)" They selected the second-best CV, which had good CV/LB correlation, and still won. — [ELL 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/feedback-prize-english-language-learning/discussion/369457)
- DAIGT 5th: did not select its best submission (0.977, which would have been 3rd) among the final three. The Mamba add-on hurt (0.972). — [DAIGT 5th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/llm-detect-ai-generated-text/discussion/470093)
- LECR 1st selected his main inference notebook "v15". His validation, not the public LB (0.727), predicted his private score (0.764). — [LECR 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/learning-equality-curriculum-recommendations/discussion/394812)
- Eedi 3rd spent two submissions on LB probing on the second-to-last day to estimate the seen:unseen ratio, then committed to a prior-shift post-process. That is a calculated risky pick backed by a measured property of the test. — [Eedi 3rd](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551498)
- Operational risk: LMSYS 1st reports "I had one sub that also failed to run because I deleted another model that I had uploaded". Freeze the artifacts for every final pick. — [LMSYS 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/lmsys-chatbot-arena/discussion/527629)
- Mixed practice in other write-ups: Playground S6E2 1st "did not choose that highest-CV submission ... because I suspected split overfitting"; Cassava 10th picked its best-LB submission plus one best-CV/high-LB submission. — search-result summaries of [Playground S6E2 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s6e2/writeups/1st-place-solution-diversity-selection-and-t) and [Cassava 10th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/cassava-leaf-disease-classification/writeups/sergey-parakhin-10th-place-solution-23rd-public) (not fetched directly)
- BELKA shows the winner's curse directly: top-5 included repeated runs of a public tutorial notebook, and the winner called his result "pure luck and randomness". With noisy metrics and many teams, the top private scores are partly selection on noise. — [Leash Bio](https://leashbio.substack.com/p/belka-results-suggest-computers-can); [BELKA 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/leash-BELKA/discussion/519020)
- CPMP on why there is a private split at all: "to avoid what is known as overfitting. Making sure they select good models and not lucky ones." — [SED 1817](https://softwareengineeringdaily.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/SED1817-Chris-Deotte-and-Jean-Francois-Puget.txt)

### Inferences
- A defensible default: Pick A is the best seed-averaged CV ensemble whose CV-LB relationship looks normal. Pick B (and C if allowed) embodies a stated hypothesis about the hidden test (bigger reranker, novel-candidate prior boost, test-time adaptation) and is also CV-supported, ideally on a CV slice that mimics the test, such as the novel-molecule fraction or prompts-per-group structure.
- With small test sets (few passages per prompt; CASMI query counts), public-LB differences under about 1-2 standard errors of fold-to-fold CV variance should be treated as noise. Simulate the public/private split with CV folds (Bhatia) to estimate this.

### Gaps
- I found no first-hand quantitative study of the winner's curse on Kaggle (e.g., expected private-minus-public drop vs. number of submissions).

## Q6. Handling a hidden test whose distribution differs from train (BELKA's new scaffolds, Eedi's unseen misconceptions, DAIGT's unknown generators)

### Takeaway
Winners (1) build validation that reproduces the novelty, (2) broaden training coverage with synthetic or external data aimed at the gap, (3) use test-time adaptation or self-supervision on test inputs, and (4) correct for the expected prior over seen vs. novel classes. Where the shift is extreme (BELKA kinase0), nobody generalized, and rankings depended on the in-distribution strata plus noise.

### Cited Findings
- Validation that reproduces novelty: BELKA 1st held out 3% of building blocks (about 9M samples with at least one non-shared block) — [BELKA 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/leash-BELKA/discussion/519020). Eedi 1st used GroupKFold by ConstructId — [Eedi 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688). Eedi 4th scored unseen-misconception subsets separately — [Eedi 4th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551559). LECR 1st excluded validation-only contents from training — [LECR 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/learning-equality-curriculum-recommendations/discussion/394812).
- Coverage via synthetic data: Eedi 1st said "The synthetic data played a crucial role in improving both raw performance and generalization capability with respect to unseen misconceptions" and kept 4k+ LLM-invented misconceptions, after deduplication, as extra candidates — [Eedi 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551688). DAIGT 1st built its data mix "to plug blindspots of the previous generation models" because the hosts had revealed little about test generators — [DAIGT 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/llm-detect-ai-generated-text/discussion/473295).
- Test-time adaptation and self-supervision on test: DAIGT 1st used a custom tokenizer, MLM and pseudo-labels on test essays ("a specialized understanding of the hidden test set") — [DAIGT 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/llm-detect-ai-generated-text/discussion/473295). DAIGT 5th used teacher → short-context-student fine-tuning on test chunks (+0.007 private) — [DAIGT 5th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/llm-detect-ai-generated-text/discussion/470093). BELKA 1st ran MLM pretraining on train+test+external SMILES — [BELKA 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/leash-BELKA/discussion/519020).
- Shift-aware features: LECR 1st built one model that excluded features "which will potentially have different distribution on real test set" and used different post-processing thresholds for train-seen vs. new contents — [LECR 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/learning-equality-curriculum-recommendations/discussion/394812).
- Prior correction: Eedi 3rd boosted unseen candidates to a 75% top-1 share, worth +0.036 private — [Eedi 3rd](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/eedi-mining-misconceptions-in-mathematics/discussion/551498). LLM 20Q 1st modelled the private keyword prior from public-keyword statistics — [LLM 20Q 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/llm-20-questions/discussion/531106).
- Limits: Leash says "We don't think anyone is capable of generalizing to new chemical space yet". "Many reported ML-generated successes might stem from building block or chemical motif memorization". They also observed that kinase0 picks were "within the range of picking random molecules". — [Leash Bio](https://leashbio.substack.com/p/belka-results-suggest-computers-can)
- More pretraining data is not automatically better: BELKA 1st spent "about a month experimenting with ZINC" pretraining without gains — [BELKA 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/leash-BELKA/discussion/519020).

### Inferences
- For CASMI's 40-50% novel molecules, build a CV with the same novel fraction, defined by structure or scaffold rather than by spectrum ID. Monitor MRR@25 separately on seen and novel queries. Calibrate any seen-vs-novel candidate bias on that CV. Generalization levers worth ablating, in order: candidate-side pretraining or self-supervision over the full candidate library, synthetic or augmented spectra for under-covered chemotypes, and then prior correction.
- For the authorship task, the test "shift" is more likely in prompts and topics than in classes, so group CV by prompt. Use the 2-3-passages-per-prompt structure: predictions within a prompt group can be jointly adjusted, similar to LECR's assignment-style post-processing and Eedi's prior matching, to maximize balanced accuracy.

### Gaps
- I found no public per-stratum (shared / non-shared / kinase0) numeric scores for BELKA top teams in text form.
- I found no Kaggle write-up quantifying adversarial-validation-based reweighting of training data in an NLP or retrieval setting.
